# Introduction

From the outset, I knew that the final form of this post would likely be a lot of words, which discouraged me from starting it for far too long. This blog post is going to contain four main sections: classes, activities, projects, and adventures. If any of those are not interesting to you, feel free to skip them. Each component is generally self-contained.

## Classes

For some reason, I chose not to take any prerequisites for my computer science classes this semester. That proved monumentally stupid, but at least not irreparable for my class trajectory.

### EDNS 151 (w/ lab)

Early in the morning (7:30), I took Cornerstone Design. The class is an attempt to take a group through the process of analyzing a market, talking with stakeholders, and then designing a solution to some stakeholder problem. The course suffered from trying to be too much in one class. There were lessons on sketching, CAD, drawings, BOMs, manufacturing supply chains, labor costs, stakeholder communication, and so many other things. It honestly could have been split into 4 different courses to focus on the separate parts. The other issue it suffered from was how one’s group could make or break the class. Since there was so much group work, having unmotivated (or simply inexperienced) teammates can cause the course to suck. For me, my team did not lack motivation, but I was clearly significantly ahead in terms of design engineering. This forced me into a high-delegation leadership role, where I did a lot of explaining and task assignment, as well as cleaning up mistakes made by my teammates. I did the majority of the CAD, with small modules contributed by teammates. Even with this situation, I have no complaints about my team. They were all driven individuals who contributed meaningfully and were passionate about the success of the team. An explanation of our final project is here.

### HASS 111

I also took a required ethics class called Nature and Human Values. The class was pretty easy in terms of workload, only requiring six short (2 pg) essays. The in-class conversations were more interesting. I got to have ethical and philosophical conversations with my classmates and learned that some of them have insane ideas about the world and about humanity. There was one guy who was advocating that humanity recede and only cover about an eighth of the planet’s solid surface. He didn’t explain how to achieve that, conveniently. There were a lot of people advocating for utilitarian ethics, which was quite disheartening as a deontological thinker myself. My prayer is that with more thinking on their part, they will see the error in their ways.

EBGN 321

In engineering economics, we began with the time value of money, and eventually applied that to before-tax cash flow models and after-tax cash flow models. I loved my professor, Andrew Petersen. I went to his office hours a bunch, and he eventually convinced me to put some money in the stock market, as my bank’s money market account was not outperforming inflation. To be fair to him, I chose a stock with a notoriously high beta, so keep that in mind when I say that after investing about $5,000, I was at one point down $900. Even so, I made a 2% return after 6 months and sold my position, walking away about $100 richer. Thanks, Andrew Petersen.

### MAT 225

In Differential Equations, I learned a lot of methods to solve complex problems involving higher-order calculus. The most interesting part of it was likely learning about using matrices to solve systems of linear equations. I now want to make my own linear equation system solver. That would be very helpful for Advent of Code.
CHGN 121 (w/ lab)
In Chemistry 1, I learned a lot of helpful lies and models for why materials behave the way they do. At the start of the semester, I was convinced that chemistry is a pseudoscience, and to this day I am still convinced. All chemists do is give names to things and make fake models that get switched out for ones with cooler graphics every 10 years. You can't seriously tell me that in 20 years kids are still going to be learning about sigma bonds and pi bonds. It’s all lies and fabrications. Even so, I managed to get a 100% on the fabled blue dye lab and did surprisingly well on the final, getting an A- in the class.

## Activities

### Oaks

In Oaks Christian Fellowship, I met weekly with a small group Bible study. I enjoyed going deeper with my group this semester as we studied the Psalms.

### Rock Climbing

I did my best to go rock climbing with my belay buddies twice a week. Climbing was a good way for me to stay active and get out the restlessness of my busy schedule, which involved a lot of sitting at a desk.

## Noblemen

Noblemen describes itself this way: “Originally starting in 2006, Noblemen began as a simple solution to an intergenerational problem: What does it mean to be a godly man in a culture where masculinity is increasingly obscured?” Noblemen is a serious time commitment. It bills itself as being no more than 5 hours per week, but the activities are designed to get you to invest significantly more. I probably averaged 8–10 hours over the semester, getting significantly higher at the end. Like all spiritual disciplines, Noblemen is not a silver bullet for a person’s besetting sins. What it will do is force you to spend more time in Scripture and in the company of other brothers in Christ than you were planning to. 

My experience was tainted by a conflict that I had with a guy in Noblemen, which nearly resulted in him being kicked out of the program. I’ve gotten past it now, but at the time, that conflict really messed me up, to the point that I was skipping all my classes and just wasting away all day. I’m extremely grateful to my Noblemen leader, Brian Hanneman, for checking in on me and supporting me with the truth of the gospel in that time.

### Work @ Labriola

Starting January of 2026, I got a job at the Labriola Makerspace on the School of Mines campus. I worked in the 3D printing/textiles lab. Coming from my job at the makerspace at Anythink Libraries, it was a relatively easy transition for me. My daily tasks included leading trainings for students on 3D printing and embroidery, as well as managing a 3D print queue and assisting patrons with projects. One of the highlights of working there was designing and running a rope-making workshop, a process I made another blog post about.

## Projects

### Advent of Code Patches

As tradition would have it, I made Advent of Code patches for myself and many of the friends who joined me. I made the first batch of them with the embroidery machine at the Montbello IdeaLab during winter break. My first makerspace job was actually at that IdeaLab, working for Sarah Kreiger. Going back there brought me some nice and nostalgic memories. Regardless, after I made the first couple there, I made the rest on the embroidery machine at the Labriola Makerspace.

### Custom Patches

As I was getting better at using the embroidery machine, I ran out of ideas for things to make for myself. So, like any normal person would do in my case, I turned to capitalism. I made a post on a Discord server I am in, offering my patch digitizing and manufacturing services to whoever would pay for them. My first customer was a guy named Conner, who I looked up to as an example of what I wanted my career path to look like when I was in high school. He wanted a bunch of super-nerdy patches with the logos of Linux distros, programming languages, and open-source software. I can’t flame him too much, though, because I knew what all but one of the logos was. The one hiccup was that he wanted patches Velcro-backed, so I had to buy a roll of Velcro and learn how to sew that on. It wasn’t terribly difficult, but I messed it up many times as I was trying to learn.
[Conner’s patches]

I also made some other patches for a different guy on the server, who just wanted a repeat of one of Conner’s designs. All together, I turned a profit of about $100 for about 6 hours of work. I’m calling that an absolute win.

## Adventures

### Hunting in KS
I spent New Year’s between my first two semesters at my uncle’s place in Kansas City. It was great to reconnect with my cousins and just spend some quality time with my family. After the New Year’s celebration, we drove out to “the ranch,” a bit of land that my uncle owns. Long story short, I shot and killed a deer. My brother and uncle got one as well. We spent the remainder of that day and the morning of the next butchering them, and then we left. I saved the heart of the one deer that wasn’t shot in the vitals and the tongues of all but one of them, which was shot in the spine and had to be finished off with a shot to the skull. Unfortunately, I didn’t save the hides, as I thought tanning would be ridiculously expensive. Thankfully, I met a guy at my makerspace job during the semester who will tan deer hides for $50 each, which seems more than reasonable to me. I’ll be sure to hit him up next time.

### RI3D

Before the start of the spring semester, I took part in the inaugural year of the Mines RI3D team. I knew that RI3D was something that I wanted to be involved in if I went to college for a while. We didn’t get access to manufacturing tools/machines set up, unfortunately, so we ended up only doing CAD and code for the robot. I was the only software guy there, so I spent a bunch of time setting up the FRC control system and getting all the control software up to date the first day. Also, we only had the space from 9am to 9pm. The second day, as the team finalized their mechanical design and strategy, they gave me my list of degrees of freedom and motors, and I began writing the code. Around this time, I also (with help) assembled a West Coast drive base, powered by donated NEOs and Spark Max ESCs. It seems like REV Robotics rewrites their library every season, which is honestly infuriating. Why can’t they limit the breaking changes?

### Deer Heart Steaks and Deer Tongue Tacos

When I was packing to move back to Mines, I made the executive decision to bring the weirdest cuts of meat I could. I’ve been fascinated by organ meat since I learned it was edible.
[meat in fridge]
With a silent prayer no one on the floor would call 911 when they found my vacuum-sealed meat in the freezer, I put my heart and tongues in there and waited for the right time to cook them. When I found an evening that worked for as many of my friends as possible, I invited all of them over. The prep was pretty insane and included boiling the tongues for 5 hours. I had to leave it on the stove in the common room while I was working on assignments. I made a sign asking people not to turn it off, and thankfully no one got concerned, and all I got was a text from my RA thanking me for making the floor smell nice with all the spices I was boiling the meat in. The heart steak was also an involved process, as I had to soak it in a saltwater brine to get the blood out and then rinse it in water about 15 times to get the blood out. Thankfully, I had help from my roommate Diego.
[Diego mixing the heart]
The actual dinner started off a bit rocky, as I got corn tortillas without realizing I needed to cook them before they could be served. Luckily, I was able to engineer a hot plate for tortilla mass production with some ⅛ in 6061-T6 aluminum sheet and numerous violations of dorm kitchen safety policy. After that, it was great, with folks who never thought they would eat deer meat, much less deer organ meat, giving it a try and genuinely enjoying it. It’s things like that that make me acutely aware of the goodness of meat and how it unites communities.
This may be really philosophical out of nowhere, but this is my blog, so I can write what I want. I think that hunting is extremely psychologically beneficial and grounding, and I would recommend it to nearly everyone I know. Not only does hunting force a person to face mortality in a real and visceral way, but it also connects them with a fundamental practice of their ancestors and with the reality of being a predator, which is lodged deep in the older and more foundational part of our brains. Finally, it gave me an immense gratitude, appreciation, and respect for the animals which produce our food. Behind every Big Mac, there is a massive beast, with a life that must be drained for me to consume it. This may sound dark or graphic to the uninitiated, but I would argue it is better to be aware of dark and graphic truths than to compartmentalize or ignore them. I desire to be a man of integrity, and the root of integrity is the latin integer, meaning to be one organized and cohesive whole, not a fragmented collection of contradictory behaviors, thoughts, and patterns.

### Sauceathon

I went to a Sauceathon in Utah! A Sauceathon is a hackathon put on by the same group that puts on OpenSauce, a massive science fair for people whose ideas are either too stupid or genius to get published in journals. It’s put on by a bunch of YouTube makers and engineers, including William Osman (The Big Willy), Kevin (the Backyard Scientist), and Michael Reeves. I went with my roommate Diego and a friend I had made through Oaks (a Christian ministry at Mines). We left on a Friday evening around 6 after packing up most of our mechanical gear, some food, and sleeping gear. At about 10 PM, we arrived at the dorm of one of my second cousins, named Jackson, who is a student at CMU. We had a great conversation with him and his dorm-mates until midnight, and then we hit the sack, as we were waking up early.
The next morning, we pounded some nasty protein shakes and hit the road again. Making it to Spanish Fork, UT, was mostly uneventful, though we had more great conversations and great views of the eastern slope and the Utah desert.
The hackathon was honestly a massive blur. We jumped in on CADing a design long before we thought hard to analyze the problem at hand and spent the rest of the time trying to compensate for a design with some fundamental flaws. Honestly, other people have done a better job talking about the hackathon, so I’m gonna link their videos here and leave this as is for the moment.

## Conclusion
In my second semester, I was much more comfortable with navigating the systems of campus, and as such, I got involved in more things. I’m grateful for how Noblemen connected me with a multi-year group of other Christian guys here at Mines. I’m grateful for all the neat classes I took, and especially for Andrew Petersen, the best economics professor of all time. I’ll likely continue the Advent of Code patches into the future. I found the split of 16 credit hours of classes, 2–3 high-intensity projects, and time for adventures optimal, and the only thing I’d want to change is to fit in more time for social interaction with other people outside of projects. I spent this semester productivity-maxxing, at the expense of my social, emotional, and spiritual wellbeing. In future semesters, I’ll likely attempt to weave more of that in.
